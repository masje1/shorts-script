# How Voyager still has power after almost 50 years, with no solar panels

## Facts
- 3 RTGs per Voyager made 470 W total at launch — about enough to run eight old 60‑watt light bulbs at once [1]
- Sunlight at Jupiter is only 3.7% as strong as at Earth (50 W/m² vs 1,361 W/m²), and at Neptune just 0.11% — like moving a desk lamp from right over your book to the far end of a football field [2][3]
- Inside each generator, the plutonium side runs about 1,000°C and the space-facing side about 300°C — the hot side is roughly four times hotter than a kitchen oven's highest setting, and even the "cold" side would still burn you [1]
- 312 silicon-germanium metal-pair junctions (thermocouples) turn that heat gap straight into current, with no moving parts to wear out [1][4]
- Only about 6.5% of the heat becomes electricity (down to 5.9% after 14 years) — like lighting only 6 bulbs out of every 100 on a string of fairy lights [1]
- Plutonium-238's half-life is 87.7 years, so power fades on a steady curve — Voyager loses about 4 watts a year and is down to roughly 210–220 W now, under half of launch power [5][6]
- In November 2026, Voyager 1 became the first human-made object a full light-day from Earth (about 25.9 billion km) — light itself, the fastest thing there is, now takes a full day to reach it [7]

## Angle
Question: How can Voyager still be making electricity after nearly 50 years in the dark, with no solar panels and no way to recharge?
Answer: It carries a chunk of plutonium that stays hot just from its own radioactivity, and rings of metal pairs wrapped around it feel one hot side and one cold side and turn that temperature gap straight into a trickle of electric current.

## Story beats
Hero: The fist-sized glowing pellet of plutonium-238 sealed inside Voyager's generators.
Problem: Past Jupiter, sunlight is too weak for solar panels (3.7% of Earth's, falling to 0.11% at Neptune), and an ordinary battery would die in weeks.
Stakes: The plutonium's heat fades on its own 87.7-year half-life, so Voyager loses about 4 watts every year; after almost 50 years it has less than half its launch power, and NASA has had to switch off instrument after instrument just to keep the radio talking.
Trick: The decaying plutonium keeps one side of the generator near 1,000°C while space cools the other side to about 300°C; 312 metal-pair junctions turn that 700°C gap directly into electricity (the Seebeck effect) — no moving parts to break.
After: Voyager 1 just crossed one light-day from Earth in November 2026, still broadcasting on a 1970s design; when its last instrument finally goes dark in the early 2030s, the spacecraft itself will keep drifting outward forever.
