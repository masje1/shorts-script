# Why these five work

Five of Harper's own published shorts, the ones he likes (confirmed 2026-11-20). Imitate the **shape**, never the phrases or the facts (the Hottest clouds line is loose physics; `iss-air` is an older cut whose subscribe ending was dropped here and must not be learned). Line lengths and timing are from the captions; spoken pace was faster than the checker's 165 wpm estimate.

What they share:
- Line 1 is the claim, concrete from the first word: never a scene-setter.
- Line 2 turns it into a question he wants answered; the answer is withheld while the mechanism builds, one step per line.
- Every number gets an everyday comparison.
- Things are named plainly and keep that name.
- Signposts ("Let me explain", "here's the crazy part") only where they follow a real question or number and lead into content.
- A late line reframes it.

## voyager.txt: How Far is Voyager 1?
- Line 1 is an extreme with a picture in it: a laser, one full day. Then speed in a comparison you can feel: 17 km/s is "twenty times faster than a sniper rifle bullet".
- "Sounds fast, but..." turns it: the duration for one light year makes the speed small.
- "But there is a catch." is earned: it is the one pivot, into the real subject (the power running out).
- Ends mid-sentence, into line 1 (a loop).

## astronauts.txt: How much do Astronauts make?
- "This is how much..." points at the screen; "No, really." answers the viewer's disbelief.
- Two signposts ("Let me explain", "here's the crazy part"), each followed straight by content (the pay scale, then Harvard).
- Comparisons carry it: a senior IRS analyst, "the rocket scientist and the tax auditor", 0.1% and "Harvard takes three".
- Loops into line 1.

## iss-air.txt: How does the ISS never run out of air?
- "This is a bottle of water." A plain flip: the ordinary object is the answer. The next line says what it really is.
- One step per line, plain words (water, air, pee); the figure is "ninety-eight out of every hundred drops".
- The reframe: "a tiny Earth, where yesterday's pee becomes tomorrow's air". Ends on a teaser line (the burning canister), no loop.

## hottest.txt: The HOTTEST planet in the Solar System
- Line 1 sets up a "but it's not": the closest planet isn't the hottest one. Line 3 is the question, then the numbers pair up (430 against 465).
- "A pressure cooker" is the everyday image for the planet.
- Two signposts, both leading into content. Loops into line 1.

## moon.txt: The Dark Side of the Moon
- A myth flip: the "dark side" isn't dark. The question ("but why?") sits at the end of line 1.
- One step per line; the mechanism (tidal locking) arrives late, after the myth is dealt with.
- No number and no loop; it ends on a wider fact. Not every short needs either.
